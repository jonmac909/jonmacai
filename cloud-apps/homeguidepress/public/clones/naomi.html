Where you probably are

You have waited years for this trip. It tends to get decided in one evening, from screenshots.

You may have watched hours of Japan videos. Many of them contradict each other, and few tell you when their numbers were checked. Meanwhile the big decisions are waiting: which month, how many cities, whether a rail pass pays, what to book first.

Those choices are easy to change on paper and hard to change once you are standing in a station. This book takes them one at a time, at the kitchen table, months before you fly.

  • Spending the trip you saved for on trains and packingThe wrong season, too many cities, a pass you did not need. Choices like these are cheap to fix at home and expensive to fix there.
  • Not keeping upThe walking, the stairs, the heat. The book opens with an honest look at what Japan asks of your body, and helps you choose a pace that suits you.
  • Being rude without knowing itShoes, baths, trains, temples. A few rules matter, and one question explains most of them.

The honest math

What one wrong decision can cost.

Nobody adds up what the kitchen-table mistakes cost, because you only find out once you are there. Here are four common ones. The book does not print prices, because they change. It teaches you to check them yourself.

Decided from screenshots

A rail pass bought because it sounded like a bargain
A pass only pays if your long journeys add up to more than its price. For some trips it does not.
Three or four cities in a week
Hotel changes cost half a day each. A day or more of the trip goes to packing and moving.
Missing the release date for things that sell out
Some tickets and tables go on sale on a fixed day. Afterwards, no hotel and no amount of money can bring them back.
A hotel in a district that empties early
Convenience-store dinners by the third night.

One idea from the book

Stop measuring Japan in miles.

On a map, Japan looks small and its cities look close. What matters is time, and time is made of rides, waits, walks, stairs and changes. A useful rule of thumb from the book: take the ride time, add about half as much again, then add the time at both ends.

Chapter 6 teaches you to think in hours. Chapter 7 turns that into the number of bases that suit a trip of seven, ten, fourteen or twenty-one nights.

The rule of thumb

A ride of up to about an hour and a half each way can make a good day trip. Beyond that, the day is mostly travelling, and it may be calmer to move your suitcase and stay the night.

Is it for you

Written for one kind of reader.

It is for you if

  • This is your first trip to Japan and you would rather be told plainly what to check than be flattered.
  • You are over fifty and want a pace, a route and a bed that suit your knees.
  • You have a folder of screenshots and no idea which of them is still true.
  • You are planning it yourself and want to make the big decisions once, in a sensible order.

It is not for you if

  • You want a ranking of the most beautiful temples or a top ten of things to eat. It is not a sightseeing guide.
  • You want current prices and train timetables printed in a book. They change, so this one teaches you to check them yourself.
  • You have been to Japan several times. You will know much of Parts Five and Six already.
  • You are moving to Japan to live, work or study.
